The exam, and how to play it

What is confirmed

  • The test has 50–100 multiple-choice questions, all subject-specific, in 2–3 hours. Negative marking of 25% per wrong answer may apply.
  • CSIR/UGC NET-JRF, DBT-JRF and similar fellowship holders are exempt from the test.
  • Interview shortlisting may depend on the test score, so the test is the gate.
  • The syllabus (2024–25 official brochure, very likely unchanged) covers ten areas: genetics, molecular techniques, biological chemistry, microbiology, ecology, plant physiology, biophysics, developmental biology, cell biology and animal physiology.

Older BITS papers used a separate 30-question aptitude section. The current notice says “all subject specific”, so aptitude questions are unlikely, but a few calculation questions are still likely within subjects.

Strategy I would use

  • Pace: about 1.8 minutes per question on average. First pass: answer everything she is sure of. Second pass: the eliminations. Leave 10 minutes at the end.
  • Guessing: with four options and a 25% penalty, even a blind guess has a small positive expected score (¼ × 1 − ¾ × 0.25 = +0.06 per question). After eliminating one option it is clearly worth answering. Skip only total blanks, and only if the paper says there are more than four options or a bigger penalty.
  • Read the stem twice for words like “not”, “except” and “incorrect”. These cost more marks than difficulty does.
  • Numericals are free marks if practised. Most are one formula (see the list below).

20 practice MCQs in the BITS style

I wrote these to match the syllabus and the level. They are not BITS questions. Answer each before opening the explanation; aim for under 30 minutes for all twenty.

Molecular biology

1. In E. coli, which enzyme removes RNA primers during replication?

  1. DNA polymerase III
  2. DNA polymerase I
  3. DNA ligase
  4. Primase
Answer
B. Polymerase I has 5′→3′ exonuclease activity that removes primers and fills the gap; ligase then seals the nick.
Genetics

2. In a population in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, 16% show a recessive phenotype. What fraction are heterozygous?

  1. 0.16
  2. 0.24
  3. 0.36
  4. 0.48
Answer
D. q² = 0.16, so q = 0.4 and p = 0.6; 2pq = 0.48.
Biochemistry

3. A competitive inhibitor changes kinetic parameters how?

  1. Km increases, Vmax unchanged
  2. Km unchanged, Vmax decreases
  3. Both decrease
  4. Km decreases, Vmax increases
Answer
A. It competes for the active site, so high substrate overcomes it; on a Lineweaver–Burk plot the lines meet on the y-axis.
Genetics

4. A test cross gives 182 parental and 18 recombinant progeny. The map distance between the genes is:

  1. 18 cM
  2. 9 cM
  3. 4.5 cM
  4. 0.9 cM
Answer
B. 18 ÷ 200 × 100 = 9 cM.
Techniques

5. Starting with 10 template copies, ideal PCR for 20 cycles gives roughly:

  1. 200 copies
  2. 2 × 10⁴ copies
  3. 1 × 10⁷ copies
  4. 1 × 10¹⁰ copies
Answer
C. 10 × 2²⁰ ≈ 10 × 1.05 × 10⁶ ≈ 1 × 10⁷.
Techniques

6. A 6-bp restriction site is expected to occur in random DNA about once every:

  1. 256 bp
  2. 1,024 bp
  3. 4,096 bp
  4. 65,536 bp
Answer
C. 4⁶ = 4,096 bp.
Gene regulation

7. The lac operon is expressed at its highest level when:

  1. Glucose present, lactose absent
  2. Glucose present, lactose present
  3. Glucose absent, lactose present
  4. Glucose absent, lactose absent
Answer
C. Lactose (allolactose) removes the repressor, and low glucose raises cAMP so CAP–cAMP activates transcription.
Cell biology

8. Phosphorylation of Rb, releasing E2F at the G1/S transition, is initiated mainly by:

  1. Cyclin B–CDK1
  2. Cyclin D–CDK4/6
  3. Cyclin A–CDK2 only
  4. p53
Answer
B. Cyclin D–CDK4/6 begins Rb phosphorylation; cyclin E–CDK2 completes it. Cyclin B–CDK1 drives mitosis.
Cell biology

9. One cycle of the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase moves:

  1. 2 Na⁺ out, 3 K⁺ in
  2. 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in
  3. 3 Na⁺ in, 2 K⁺ out
  4. 1 Na⁺ out, 1 K⁺ in
Answer
B. 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in per ATP, making the pump electrogenic.
Developmental biology

10. In the Drosophila egg, bicoid mRNA is localised at the:

  1. Posterior pole
  2. Anterior pole
  3. Dorsal side
  4. Uniformly
Answer
B. Anterior; Bicoid protein forms an anterior-to-posterior gradient. Nanos mRNA is posterior.
Developmental biology

11. Spemann’s organizer in amphibian embryos is located in the:

  1. Animal cap
  2. Ventral marginal zone
  3. Dorsal lip of the blastopore
  4. Vegetal pole
Answer
C. Transplanting the dorsal lip induces a secondary axis.
Animal physiology

12. Which shifts the oxygen–haemoglobin dissociation curve to the right?

  1. Lower temperature
  2. Higher pH
  3. Higher 2,3-BPG
  4. Lower CO₂
Answer
C. Raised 2,3-BPG, CO₂, temperature or acidity lower haemoglobin’s O₂ affinity (Bohr effect), favouring unloading in tissues.
Immunity

13. Peptides presented on MHC class I are recognised by:

  1. CD4⁺ T cells
  2. CD8⁺ T cells
  3. B cells
  4. Neutrophils
Answer
B. CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells; MHC class II presents to CD4⁺ T cells.
Microbiology

14. Lipopolysaccharide is a component of the:

  1. Gram-positive cell wall
  2. Gram-negative outer membrane
  3. Bacterial cytoplasmic membrane
  4. Fungal cell wall
Answer
B. LPS (with lipid A, the endotoxin) sits in the outer leaflet of the Gram-negative outer membrane.
Ecology

15. On average, the fraction of energy passed from one trophic level to the next is about:

  1. 1%
  2. 10%
  3. 50%
  4. 90%
Answer
B. Lindeman’s 10% rule; the rest is lost mostly as respiration and heat.
Plant physiology

16. In C4 plants, the first CO₂ acceptor in mesophyll cells is:

  1. Ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate
  2. Phosphoenolpyruvate
  3. Oxaloacetate
  4. 3-Phosphoglycerate
Answer
B. PEP carboxylase fixes CO₂ onto PEP to give oxaloacetate, the first product.
Biophysics

17. The quickest common method to estimate a protein’s α-helix and β-sheet content in solution is:

  1. X-ray crystallography
  2. Circular dichroism
  3. Mass spectrometry
  4. SDS-PAGE
Answer
B. CD in the far-UV gives characteristic spectra for helix and sheet. X-ray gives full structure but needs crystals.
Biochemistry

18. An acetate buffer (pKa 4.76) is at pH 5.76. The ratio [acetate]/[acetic acid] is:

  1. 0.1
  2. 1
  3. 10
  4. 100
Answer
C. pH − pKa = 1, so the ratio is 10¹ = 10.
Techniques

19. An NADH solution reads A₃₄₀ = 0.311 in a 1 cm cuvette (ε = 6220 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹). Its concentration is:

  1. 5 µM
  2. 50 µM
  3. 500 µM
  4. 5 mM
Answer
B. c = A ÷ εl = 0.311 ÷ 6220 = 5 × 10⁻⁵ M = 50 µM.
Molecular biology

20. Double-stranded DNA contains 30% adenine. Its guanine content is:

  1. 20%
  2. 30%
  3. 40%
  4. 70%
Answer
A. A = T = 30%, so G + C = 40% and G = 20%.

Interview guide

BITS does not publish an interview format, so this is based on how department PhD panels in India commonly run. Expect a panel of several faculty from the department, roughly 15–30 minutes, starting easy and then probing whatever she says. The panel is testing three things: does she understand the basics properly, does she think like a scientist, and does she genuinely want this.

1. Her own story (prepare word for word)

  • Introduce yourself in 60–90 seconds: degree, specialization, dissertation in one line, why research.
  • Dissertation in five beats: the question, why it mattered, what she did, what she found, what she would do next. Then expect “why that method?”, “what were your controls?” and “what went wrong?”.
  • Why a PhD now, and why BITS Hyderabad (name the faculty and research she read about).
  • Which area, and what she will do if her first-choice lab is not available. Answer with flexibility, not desperation.

2. Fundamentals they are likely to probe

  • Central dogma and its exceptions (reverse transcription, ribozymes, prions)
  • How PCR works; no band or multiple bands, what are the likely causes?
  • Western blot steps and loading controls
  • Apoptosis versus necrosis; cell-cycle checkpoints
  • RNAi versus CRISPR knockout: when would you choose each?
  • Positive versus negative controls, with an example from her own work
  • One signalling pathway end to end (for example MAPK or insulin)
  • Innate versus adaptive immunity, if immunology is her stated interest

3. The “design an experiment” question

A common prompt: “How would you test whether gene X is needed for cell migration?” Answer in this order:

  1. Hypothesis in one sentence.
  2. Model system and why (a cell line or an organism).
  3. Perturbation: knockdown or knockout, plus a rescue to prove specificity.
  4. Readout: wound-healing or transwell assay, quantified.
  5. Controls: non-targeting siRNA or guide, untreated cells.
  6. Statistics: replicates and the test she would use.

4. How to handle hard moments

  • Don’t know? Say so plainly, then reason aloud: “I’m not sure, but I would expect … because …”. Panels reward reasoning over bluffing.
  • Never list a technique she has not done herself as hands-on experience; “I understand the principle” is fine.
  • Pause before answering. Five seconds of thinking looks confident, not slow.
  • Keep answers to about a minute, then stop and let them steer.
